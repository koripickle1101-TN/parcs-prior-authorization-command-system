01

Why I Built PARCS

Prior authorization interests me because the patient often feels the delay without seeing the administrative chain behind it.

What I am studying

How eligibility, authorization requirements, documentation, payer follow-up, aging, escalation, and service clearance stay connected.

What the patient may experience

Waiting, uncertainty, repeated calls, a changed appointment, or being told that something is still pending.

What I am practicing

Tracing a visible delay or denial risk back to the earlier workflow condition that made it possible.

The question I keep asking

Where did the authorization workflow first lose control?

An authorization status is a signal. I want to understand the workflow that produced it.

NEW CONTROL

Pre-Service Requirement Detection Gate™ + Authorization Exception Review™

PARCS now begins before the request itself. The interactive workspace first tests whether the authorization/referral requirement was identified from an authoritative source, whether the service context is aligned, whether documentation is ready, and whether ownership is clear before the workflow advances.

Operational distinction

The first control is requirement detection—not submission.

PARCS now models the full operational path: requirement detection → source verification → readiness → ownership → submission → active follow-up → determination → closure.

02A

Requirement-to-Workflow Trace

PARCS begins by identifying the requirement that creates the authorization obligation and tracing where that requirement first becomes operational. The requirement may affect documentation, submission timing, payer communication, work queues, escalation, status tracking, and patient access. This separates the upstream requirement from the downstream point where a delay or failure eventually becomes visible.

Operations-intelligence question: Where did the policy, payer, contractual, or organizational requirement first become an operational consequence?

02B

Benefit / Service-to-Authorization Requirement Gate

PARCS starts with the supported requirement, not with submission. The workflow checks whether the service appears to require authorization according to the authoritative source used in the simulated case, documents that source, confirms service context and readiness, and then advances the case through submission, follow-up, determination, and closure.

Evidence boundary

PARCS does not independently determine coverage, medical necessity, coding correctness, or payer policy. When the available source does not support a requirement, the simulated workflow records the uncertainty and routes the case for review rather than inventing a rule.

08

What I Learned

Building PARCS reinforced that a late authorization problem can be the visible end of a much earlier workflow breakdown.

Failure

An earlier requirement, documentation, eligibility, or ownership issue is missed.

The problem may enter the workflow before the authorization itself becomes visibly at risk.

Signal

The case becomes pending, denied, not started, or close to the service date.

The visible status is useful, but it does not automatically explain the root cause.

Patient impact

The downstream effect may feel much simpler than the internal workflow.

From the patient side, the result may just feel like waiting, uncertainty, another call, or a changed appointment.

Patient-to-professional insight

The patient experiences the downstream consequence.

The operations team has to determine whether the authorization requirement became visible early enough for someone to act on it. The first loss of control may have happened at requirement detection—not at submission or denial.

Integrity Boundary

This project has not been deployed in a healthcare organization. I do not claim that PARCS has produced real-world denial reductions, authorization turnaround improvements, cost savings, productivity gains, or patient outcomes.
